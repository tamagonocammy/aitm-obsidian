> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Family:** Twin of [[Syayvi]]
> **Personality Snapshot:** Quiet, introspective, with severe dissociation. Developed alter **Kerevi** after killing father to spare dying mother. Once destined to be tsakarem and Kaymi's mate - calling severed by exile. Tìmeyo appears withdrawn, struggles under stress; Kerevi emerges as articulate, joyful presence. Mangkwan's heretical rituals under Varang fractured her connection further - Tìmeyo recoiled, Kerevi found disturbing fascination. Firmly loyal to [[Syayvi]] but harbors unspoken doubts about rejecting traditions. Soul torn between duty to new path and longing for spiritual connection.

## Profile

**Personality:** Tìmeyo is quiet and withdrawn, carrying herself with a stoic exterior that masks profound emotional fragility. She struggles to express herself properly, her few carefully chosen words often landing wrong—perceived as blunt or insensitive despite genuine care. When stress becomes overwhelming, she doesn't break down but simply *stops*, dissociating so completely she becomes functionally nonverbal, lost somewhere deep inside where the world cannot reach her. This is when **Kerevi** emerges: a fully distinct alter within their DID system, born during the killing of their father when that trauma became too immense for one consciousness to bear alone. Where Tìmeyo is paralyzed by emotion, Kerevi is present and confident. But she's also deeply *childish* not in body but in spirit. She speaks in a patchwork of borrowed phrases collected from everyone Tìmeyo has known: Syayvi's commanding declarations, Kaymi's protective instincts, Eyfuwen's storytelling cadences, even Varang's twisted rhetoric and Quaritch's military efficiency. She mimics unconsciously, cobbling together a personality from fragments because she was born with no template for how to be. "Why are we walking?" she'll ask when a destination is fifty paces away. "We have ikran. Why wouldn't we fly?" Her logic is the simple directness of a child who cannot comprehend why adults complicate everything. This creates constant friction. She'll use Txafwe's teaching phrases back at him with childish enthusiasm, then pivot to Rängay's stern corrections mid-sentence, completely unaware that this verbal whiplash disturbs others. When frustrated, she becomes genuinely petulant—crossing her arms, tail lashing, muttering "This is stupid" like an irritated child. Her problem-solving is dangerously simplistic: "If the RDA wants this territory, we kill all of them. Problem solved." Not cruelty but just a child's if-then logic without understanding consequence or complexity. Most unsettling was her relationship with the Mangkwan’s clan heresy. Where Tìmeyo recoiled in spiritual horror, Kerevi approached Varang's corrupted rituals with a child's dangerous curiosity, not belief but fascination. "Oh, so if you twist the prayer this way, people obey. Interesting." She participated with the concentration of someone learning a new game, not understanding that some games destroy souls. She survived captivity by pattern recognition and pleasing authority figures, memorizing which prayers kept them safe without comprehending *why* it mattered. But beneath the childishness lies fierce protectiveness. "*I'm Tìmeyo's savior,*" she says with absolute conviction. Her entire existence is rooted in one purpose: be what Tìmeyo cannot. Be strong when Tìmeyo is weak, present when Tìmeyo is lost, able to *act* when Tìmeyo is paralyzed. During the **Siege of the Falls**, this dynamic reached its peak. Kerevi fronted with increasing frequency during combat, thriving in situations with clear rules: enemy approaches, you stop them. No moral ambiguity, just direct action. She fought with borrowed techniques: Kaymi's strikes, Tsu'lan's stances, even Mangkwan formations, cobbled into something uniquely effective, alongside a childish, but chilling laughter. Fighting alongside Syayvi to protect the children, both alters worked in genuine tandem: Tìmeyo's loyalty providing the *why*, Kerevi's clarity providing the *how*. The final strike against Luka forced them both to understand a fundamental truth: redemption wasn't about integration or Tìmeyo "getting better." They were both real, both necessary, both part of the same person who survived by becoming plural. The Siege didn't heal their DID, but it taught them functional coexistence as a system. Now they've reached uneasy equilibrium. Tìmeyo still carries spiritual yearning for their lost calling as tsakarem, deep connection to Syayvi, unspoken doubts about their exile. Kerevi still speaks in borrowed phrases with childish directness that frustrates everyone, still lacks social grace, still approaches problems with simplistic logic. But they're learning to work together, switching based on what situations require rather than fighting for control. Among the Scattered Ashes, everyone has learned to identify who's fronting: Tìmeyo is quiet and careful, Kerevi is loud and present. Syayvi loves both versions of her twin, speaking gently with Tìmeyo and setting clear boundaries with Kerevi, who responds well to structure. Watching Syayvi prepare for motherhood affects them differently, Tìmeyo feels complex grief about ever feeling whole enough to create life, while Kerevi feels uncomplicated excitement: "We're going to have a baby! Can I hold it?" Despite developing her own identity and fierce autonomy, Kerevi has never tried to permanently take control. Even in Tìmeyo's darkest dissociative episodes, Kerevi dives into the internal abyss to pull her back, her childlike joy and relief when Tìmeyo resurfaces—*"I found you! You came back!"*—revealing the truth: they need each other. Kerevi cannot exist without Tìmeyo's core self to give her meaning. Tìmeyo cannot survive their trauma without Kerevi's protection. They are one person with DID, learning to move through a world that doesn't understand what that means. They are proof that survival sometimes means fracturing, that healing doesn't always mean becoming singular, that strength can look like accepting your own multiplicity. They are adapted, not broken. And in a world as harsh as theirs, adaptation is its own form of grace.
