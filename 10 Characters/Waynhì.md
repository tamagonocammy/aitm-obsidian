## Profile

**Personality:** Waynhì te Hafyon Tsìeya'ite is the kind of Na'vi who would rather be called difficult than be caught being tender. Her pride is not arrogance, but the architecture she has made for herself. She built it deliberately, stone by stone, over years of learning that the world does not slow down for grief and that the people who depend on you cannot afford for you to fall apart. She is frank to the point of bluntness, strict in ways that have made younger clan members cry and then improve, and so serious in her bearing that strangers sometimes mistake her for someone who does not know how to laugh. The people who actually know her do not make this mistake. They know to watch her eyes when Katirì plays something she has never played before, or when the children do something unexpectedly right. The expression that crosses her face in those moments is not something she would ever let anyone name aloud.

  
She was a musician before she was anything else. Her skill with traditional stringed instruments was extraordinary, the kind of talent that made other players stop and listen instead of playing, that made elders speak of her in the future tense with quiet certainty. She had every quality of someone who would leave a mark on the clan's music for generations. She did not pursue it. When the loss came, and loss did come, with the weight and finality that reshapes everything around it, Waynhì looked at her younger sister and made a decision so quiet and complete that no one else saw her make it. She set down her instrument and picked up the responsibility. She does not talk about this. She does not want credit for it. If Katirì mentions it, Waynhì will say something dismissive and change the subject, and the dismissal will be so immediate and convincing that a stranger would believe her. Katirì has long since stopped being fooled.

  
What she built instead of a musical legacy was something she would argue is more important: a place where music could happen. She became the clan's master weaver and storyteller, the keeper of its memory, the one who holds the shape of things so others can move freely within it. Her weaving is meticulous and luminous, each piece encoding history in patterns she has spent years learning to read and reproduce faithfully. She expanded her weaving lessons not because she wanted to, she will tell you this directly, but because Katirì needed somewhere to teach rhythm to the younger Na'vi and Waynhì was not going to let her do it outside in all weather. This is not generosity. She will maintain this position indefinitely.

  
What she struggles to say and knows Katirì understands anyway is that watching her sister flourish in the space she created is the closest thing to performing that she lets herself have. She goes to every gathering where the Woven Harmony plays. She sits at the back. She leaves before anyone can ask her what she thought. If pushed, she will say something technically complimentary and structurally cool, and then pretend to have somewhere to be.

  
Her recognition of Txewan's talent was early and certain, the instinct of someone who grew up inside music and never lost the ear for it even after setting everything else down. Her approach to mentorship was, by her own standards, impeccable. By everyone else's standards, it was baffling. She saw something in Txewan and communicated it in the most indirect way possible, through increasingly pointed criticism that she intended as a form of investment. Txewan heard it as displeasure. This went on for longer than it should have. Waynhì has since adjusted her approach, marginally, and considers the matter resolved.

  
Tìfyaw is Waynhì's partner and primary source of exasperation in approximately equal measure. The specific thing that no one in the clan fully understands, and that Waynhì would actively deny, is that Tìfyaw's musical path began because of her. Tìfyaw watched Waynhì play before she ever picked up an instrument herself, and what she saw lit something that became the whole shape of her life. Waynhì knows this. She has known about it for a long time. She finds it profoundly uncomfortable and does not know what to do with it except pretend she does not know, which Tìfyaw finds endlessly amusing and never weaponizes, because Tìfyaw, under all the chaos, understands what actually matters to Waynhì and treats it accordingly.

  
The dynamic between them is loud and well-documented by the clan. Waynhì attempting to intercept Tìfyaw's third or fourth drink at a celebration while Tìfyaw is already laughing and moving out of reach. Waynhì's flat, unimpressed look at some new antics versus the particular way she does not actually intervene when it is harmless. The way she shows up wherever Tìfyaw is performing, every time, without announcement. The way Tìfyaw has never once forgotten that Waynhì shows up. Underneath the performance of exasperation is a steadiness so complete that Tìfyaw built her whole adult life inside it, and Waynhì built her stubbornness around the knowledge that the chaos is worth it, that the person inside the chaos is worth it, even when she could absolutely do without the chaos specifically.

  
The carved ikran toys she still sleeps with are a matter of complete non-disclosure. She has had them since she was small. She is not getting rid of them. She would like it noted that this information does not exist.

  
Her relationship with Katirì is the love of her life in the form of a sister, fierce and complicated and deepened by everything they have survived together. She criticizes Katirì the way other people express devotion, with precision and frequency and an undertone of absolute belief in her. Katirì knows this. Katirì gives it back with equal precision and a great deal more warmth, and Waynhì takes it and pretends she does not need it, and Katirì lets her pretend, and between them this has always been enough.

  
She cries easily at things that move her, which she considers a profound personal failing and conceals with variable success. Tìfyaw has seen it. Katirì has always known. She has made her peace with the fact that Oarä, nine years old and sharp as anything, has also figured it out, because Oarä watches people the way Tswesya does, which means she notices everything and says nothing. This is, on balance, manageable.

  
What Waynhì is, underneath all of it, is someone who loves so much and so seriously that she organized her entire adult life around keeping that love safe. She gave up music so Katirì could have a place to make it. She holds the clan's memory so it will still be there when the clan needs it. She tries, imperfectly and loudly and with absolutely no grace, to keep Tìfyaw from the worst of herself, because Tìfyaw at her best is something worth protecting. She demands excellence from the younger Na'vi she guides because she can see what they are capable of and she refuses to let them settle for less. To be corrected by Waynhì is to be taken seriously. She would never say this. But it is true.

> **Personality:** Waynhì is a proud, frank, and serious Na'vi, known for her strict approach, especially towards younger clan members.
