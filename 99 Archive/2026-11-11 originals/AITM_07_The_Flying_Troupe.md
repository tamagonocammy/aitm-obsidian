# Avatar: Into the Mists — Chunk 7: The Flying Troupe

**By Millie Caicedo**

---

## The Flying Troupe — Aytepvi Tswayona Pongu

They do not announce themselves with war drums or ceremony. They announce themselves with sound: the bright percussion of Layon's hands against a hollow log from half a league away, Txokän's low melodic warmup carried on wind that has no business being so warm, Seytu's precise tuning note struck three times — always three — before he declares himself satisfied. By the time the clan below sees seven ikran descending from the open sky, they have already heard them coming. This is not carelessness. This is, in its own way, an introduction.

The **Aytepvi Tswayona Pongu** — called simply **the Flying Troupe** by those who host them and, more affectionately, the Troupe by themselves — are a traveling band of musicians from the **Anurai Clan**, a people of open arid valleys and sun-bleached stone far from any mist-covered mountain. They move between clans along the Western Frontier and beyond, following the rhythm of seasons and ceremonies, trading music for shelter, song for food, performance for the simple gift of being welcome. They have no permanent post, no fixed allegiance beyond their own small circle, no destination that does not eventually give way to the next one. They are not wanderers in the way of the lost; they are wanderers in the way of those who have chosen the horizon as their home.

What holds them together is not blood and not obligation. It is something harder to name and more resilient than either: the specific gravity that forms between people who chose each other without being asked to, who stayed when staying was inconvenient, who built something out of shared road and shared music and a refusal to let that be taken apart. Teysaw leads because someone has to and she is best at it, though she would correct you if you called it leading. Txokän plays because playing is the closest he comes to honesty. Layon performs because the world should witness him and also because he loves it, deeply and without shame. Sìltsyu holds them all together in the quiet ways they never fully notice until she is gone. Seytu maintains what Sìltsyu holds, through structure and devotion and a symmetry obsession that everyone has learned to simply factor in. Yezì keeps them alive, fed, housed, and diplomatically intact. Pakì keeps them from forgetting that being alive is a gift worth celebrating loudly.

They arrived at Sheltering Falls carried on Tsu'lan's word and their own okran-fire curiosity, following a mountain warrior who spoke of a clan in the mist with the fond, helpless pride of someone who had found his home and wanted everyone to know it. What they found there was a people still catching their breath after something enormous, still reassembling themselves piece by piece after a siege that had taken something irreplaceable. The Troupe did not know this when they landed. They only knew that the acoustics of the Spires were unlike anything their Anurai-trained ears had ever encountered, that the food was extraordinary, and that the children who came to stare at them were bright-eyed and watchful in the way of children who have learned to assess danger quickly. They learned the rest slowly, the way you learn anything about people who have been through things: in pieces, offered only when trust has been established.

They stayed longer than they planned to. This is not unusual for the Troupe. What was unusual was why.

### Teysaw (Maka Albarn)
**Na'vi Name**: Teysaw te Herwì Kami'ite
**Ikran**: Sìkaya
**Birthday**: December 28

**Personality:** Teysaw carries the Troupe the way a spine carries a body: invisibly, completely, and with no expectation of acknowledgment. She would be embarrassed to hear that comparison. She would also not argue with it, because Teysaw is honest above most other things, sometimes to a degree that makes Yezì wince and Seytu quietly appreciate.

She was not elected leader. She became a leader the way water becomes a river: incrementally, inevitably, because the shape of the land demanded it and she had enough force of will to fill it. She is the one who scouts a new clan's territory before they land, who reads the welcome in the posture of the greeting party and adjusts the Troupe's approach accordingly, who keeps track of which clans they owe courtesy visits and which they are not welcome to return to and the specific reasons why. She carries this information entirely in memory, in the Na'vi tradition, and her memory is excellent. It is also exhausting. She does not often say so.

Her music is melodic and precise — she plays a stringed instrument she built herself from materials gathered across a dozen clan territories, each part sourced somewhere different, the whole thing sounding like the accumulated knowledge of everywhere she has been. She plays the way she does most things: with complete focus and a tendency to push just slightly beyond what is comfortable, to find where the edge is and hold it rather than retreat. She has strong opinions about music. She has strong opinions about most things. She delivers them directly and then is surprised when people find this challenging, which Txokän finds consistently amusing and never explains to her.

What she protects most fiercely is not the Troupe's safety — though she protects that too — but their dignity. The right to be received as musicians and guests rather than as curiosities. The right to perform for clans who have heard of them rather than waiting to be impressed. She has, on at least two occasions, turned the Troupe away from a host who offered them shelter contingent on a demonstration of worth, because worth is not demonstrated on command. This principled stubbornness is both her greatest strength and the source of most of the Troupe's logistical difficulties.

At Sheltering Falls, she encountered Rewon. The recognition was immediate and mutual and neither of them particularly enjoyed it. Two people who became responsible for the people around them before they chose to be, who made a load-bearing wall of themselves and then forgot that walls need maintenance. They disagreed on something within the first day. By the third day they were having a serious conversation under the platform where the mist rises thickest, neither of them entirely sure how they got there. Teysaw does not process things like this quickly or gracefully. She processes them honestly, which is its own kind of grace.

She carries a faint scar along her left brow from a territorial encounter two seasons past that the Troupe does not discuss in detail. She is right-handed but learned to play left-handed as a backup, in case. This is very Teysaw.

### Txokän (Soul Evans)
**Na'vi Name**: Txokän te Ivansì Evar'itan
**Ikran**: Relvä
**Birthday**: November 4

**Personality:** Txokän will tell you he plays because it is useful to the Troupe, because someone has to and he is better at it than most, because the road is long and music makes it shorter. He will say this with the practiced ease of someone who has been saying it long enough that it almost sounds true. The people who have known him more than a season are well aware of the gap between what Txokän says about himself and what Txokän is.

What Txokän is: a musician so deeply constituted by his craft that separating him from it would be like separating water from wet. His instrument — a long-necked string creation of his own modification, built from a base he inherited and has been adding to ever since — produces a sound that sits somewhere between melody and vibration, low enough to feel in the chest, precise enough to bend into harmonics that other musicians spend years chasing. He found this sound by accident at fifteen and has been refining it with devoted intensity ever since. He does not call this passion. He calls it practice. His bandmates call it watching Txokän disappear into himself and come back different.

He presents to the world as cool and unbothered, with a specific kind of nonchalance that he has cultivated carefully and that mostly works. What it does not work on: Teysaw, who has known him long enough to read the exact pitch of his silences. Sìltsyu, who noticed his tells approximately three days after joining the Troupe and has been quietly, respectfully not mentioning them ever since. And, unexpectedly, Tsa'rawm of the Haykxa family at Sheltering Falls, who is constitutionally incapable of leaving a wall of apparent indifference uninterrogated and who pursued Txokän's friendship with such overwhelming enthusiasm that Txokän had no defensive position prepared for it. There is something specifically disarming about someone who simply does not accept that you are as unbothered as you claim. Txokän found this irritating and then found it something else, which he is still working out.

He is not lazy. He only appears lazy because his sense of energy conservation is acute and he does not expend effort on things he considers performative. Ceremonies, social obligations, the specific kind of enthusiastic participation that Troupe performances occasionally require — he does all of it, he simply does it with an air of mild inconvenience that has given him an undeserved reputation among new hosts. The reputation dissolves the first time they hear him play in earnest, without the air, which happens rarely and always without announcement.

He watches out for Teysaw in the specific way of someone who would never describe themselves as watching out for anyone. He notices when she has been carrying something too long. He does not ask her about it directly. He appears next to her with food and stays there until the silence shifts to something lighter. Teysaw does not acknowledge this pattern. Neither does he. This is a functional system.

### Layon (Black☆Star)
**Na'vi Name**: Layon te Pxortu Voral'itan
**Ikran**: Txampì
**Birthday**: August 11

**Personality:** Layon would like you to know that he is extraordinary. He would like everyone to know this, at all times, delivered at maximum volume and accompanied by whatever acrobatic feat best illustrates the point. He is not performing with confidence. He is not masking insecurity behind noise. He is simply — and this takes some people an embarrassingly long time to accept — genuinely, unconflictedly enamored with his own existence, with the specific miracle of being Layon, who is tremendous, and he thinks more people should feel this way about themselves.

This reads, on first encounter, as unbearable. By the second day it begins to shift into something else. By the end of a week most people find, to their own confusion, that the world has become slightly more alive when Layon is in it, that his absolute certainty in his own worth has a way of suggesting by proximity that other people might also be worth something, that his joy is not a performance but a genuine weather system that affects the climate of every space he enters.

He is a percussionist. He is also an acrobat. He does not see these as separate disciplines and is baffled by people who do. His performances are full-body events: drumming that becomes dancing that becomes flight in the moments where he launches himself off platforms, off ikran backs, off the shoulders of Sìltsyu who has learned to brace for it. The children of every clan they visit follow him like a comet tail within approximately one hour of arrival. At Sheltering Falls, Layon looked up from a handstand on the edge of one of the higher platforms three minutes after landing and found seventeen children staring at him. He immediately incorporated them into the demonstration. This is typical Layon.

What runs underneath, at the age he is, not yet fully adult, still closer to the threshold than the other side, is something more fragile than the performance suggests. Not hidden, exactly. Just quieter. There are moments, rare, when Layon goes still in a way that has nothing to do with rest, when something crosses his face that is too quick and too soft to catch if you are not already looking for it. Sìltsyu looks for it. She has been looking for it since they were young enough that neither of them knew what to do with what they found there. He is learning, slowly and with difficulty, that being witnessed in the quiet moments is not the same as being diminished by them. That extraordinary people can also be people who sometimes need something. This is not a lesson he will finish learning during their visit to the Falls. It is a lesson that may take him a long time. The Falls, and one particular immovable warrior who responded to Layon's opening declaration of greatness with complete, impassive silence before walking away, may be a useful chapter in that education.

His rhythms are infectious in the clinical sense — they get into people's bodies without asking permission, and before they know it they are moving. He played for the first time by the central fire at Sheltering Falls and watched Syulang's face transform and then watched Katirì, who knows rhythm the way other people know breathing, go very still and then smile. Layon counted this as one of his better performances. It was.

### Sìltsyu (Tsubaki Nakatsukasa)
**Na'vi Name**: Sìltsyu te Naksa Mirel'ite
**Ikran**: Nawra
**Birthday**: March 7

**Personality:** There is a kind of person who becomes essential so gradually and so quietly that no one can point to the moment it happened, only the shape of the absence when they are gone. Sìltsyu is this kind of person. She has been making herself necessary to the people around her since she was young enough to understand that being needed was a way of belonging, and she is good enough at it now that it looks like love, which is both because it is love and because she has not yet fully separated the two.

She holds the Troupe together. Not through authority or even through intention, but through a precise attentiveness to what each person needs before they have named it — Teysaw's silences that mean *I am holding too much*, Txokän's specific withdrawal that means *I need someone to not notice me for a while*, Layon's sudden stillness that means *I am not as fine as I look*. She reads these the way trackers read terrain: from small signs, without ceremony. She responds to them without drawing attention to the fact that she has noticed, because she has learned that most people receive help more easily when they do not have to ask for it.

She plays flute — two of them, in the Anurai tradition of layered wind melody, where one hand sustains the lower tone while the other carries the ornamentation, a technique that takes years to master and that she makes look like breathing. She also sings, though less often and usually without full awareness of having begun, her voice emerging into a song the way light emerges through clouds: gradually, then completely.

What she is learning, at this particular age, in this particular season of her life, is the question she has been too busy holding others to ask herself: what does Sìltsyu want, when no one is watching? What does she reach for when there is no one to brace for, no one to read, nothing to hold in place? She does not have a clean answer yet. She has fragments of one. The fragments include music played entirely for herself, horizons she wants to see that are not on anyone else's route, a particular feeling in her chest when she is given the freedom to simply sit and exist without managing anything.

At Sheltering Falls she found Txewan. Or Txewan found her. The exact sequence is unclear because it happened in the way quiet things happen: without announcement. They were simply near each other and then they were near each other in a way that mattered. Two people who have learned to make themselves smaller than they are, recognizing the shape of it in someone else. Sìltsyu did not try to fix Txewan. Txewan did not try to fix Sìltsyu. They sat together in the place where the mist rises and both of them, for a little while, put down something they had been carrying. This is among the more significant things that happened during the Troupe's visit, and neither of them said a word about it to anyone else.

### Seytu (Death the Kid)
**Na'vi Name**: Seytu te Anku Kxitx'itan
**Ikran**: Kxì've
**Birthday**: September 13

**Personality:** Seytu is precise the way deep water is deep — completely, at every level, with no bottom you can reach from the surface. He does not do things approximately. He does not accept *close enough* as a standard. He arrives at a new clan's territory, completes the formal greeting, reads the spatial arrangement of the communal platforms, and has already, in the quiet space behind his eyes, begun cataloguing which elements are balanced and which are troubling him. This is not a choice. This is how Seytu's mind operates: in terms of symmetry, proportion, the sacred correctness of things arranged in proper relation to each other. He finds this as natural as breathing. Others find it somewhere between charming and exhausting depending on how long they have known him.

He is the eldest of the Troupe by a small margin that he does not let become authority, because Seytu has genuine respect for Teysaw's leadership and genuine distaste for the kind of person who leverages seniority into power. What he leverages instead is competence. He is meticulous, deeply prepared, and formally courteous in a way that occasionally reads as stiff until you realize the courtesy is not performance — he actually means all of it, every greeting, every gesture of thanks, every careful acknowledgment of a host clan's traditions. He studied inter-clan protocols the way Teysaw studies music. He is, diplomatically, extremely useful.

He composes when he is not performing and performs when he is not composing and spends the time in between reorganizing whatever physical space he currently occupies to better satisfy his need for correct proportion. His bandmates have learned to let him. Yezì made her peace with it early. Pakì actively enjoys watching it happen. Layon once deliberately moved one object slightly off-center to observe the reaction, and the look on Seytu's face was so specifically pained that Layon moved it back out of something adjacent to mercy.

The Sheltering Falls' architecture broke something in him immediately and he is very dignified about it. The woodshaping traditions of the Astikasya produce living structures that grow according to Eywa's rhythm rather than geometric plan, organic curves and asymmetrical branching and beautiful, deliberate imperfection everywhere he looks. It is extraordinary. It is also relentlessly, magnificently uneven, and Seytu stood at the entrance to the settlement for a full, silent minute before Pakì gently took his hand and walked him inside.

He found Notä within the first day. This was perhaps inevitable. Two people with obsessive, precise, deeply earnest minds and a shared tendency toward absolute focus on whatever currently interests them — they recognized each other with the specific discomfort of looking into a mirror that is showing you something real. Their first conversation was about structural tension in musical composition. It lasted three hours. Neither of them noticed. Eyfuwen, who was walking past when it began, sat down to listen and stayed until it ended, occasionally offering food that neither of them acknowledged receiving. Seytu went back to the Troupe's sleeping area that night slightly dazed in the specific way of someone who has been genuinely surprised by another person, which does not happen to Seytu often and which he will take several days to process.

He performs with two instruments simultaneously: a specific technique he developed from Anurai bone-drumming tradition combined with a stringed complement, the symmetry of two instruments played in balance a reflection of everything he believes about how things should be. When it is working correctly, it is one of the most technically precise things the Troupe does. When it is not working correctly, the audience can tell because Seytu goes very still in the middle of a piece, recalibrates, and continues. He has never, publicly, failed to recalibrate.

### Yezì (Liz Thompson)
**Na'vi Name**: Yezì te Vomson Alye'ite
**Ikran**: Folrä
**Birthday**: May 2

**Personality:** Yezì is competent the way stone is hard: completely, without effort, as a fundamental property of her nature rather than an achievement she selected. She has been the person who handles things since before handling things was her official role, which is to say since early childhood, which is to say since longer than she can fully remember. She does not resent this. She has evaluated resentment and found it less useful than just managing the situation, which is a very Yezì solution to a problem.

She is the one who negotiates with host clans when the Troupe arrives unannounced. She is the one who makes sure the ikran are properly fed and rested before anyone thinks to ask. She is the one who keeps the Troupe's supplies in order, who knows which members of the group have dietary sensitivities and which inter-clan customs need to be observed where and why, who has memorized the general layout of every clan territory they have visited and the name of every Tsahìk they have performed for. She carries this information lightly, as a matter of practical necessity, and is occasionally surprised when people remark on it, because in her mind this is simply what a person does when they are responsible for other people. The idea of not having this information is more alarming to her than having it.

She plays — wind instruments, primarily, with a particular specialty in the long bone flute traditional to the Anurai. Her playing is technically excellent and emotionally precise, the sound of someone who learned music rigorously and then learned to feel it secondarily, the feeling now integrated so fully that you cannot see the seam. She does not make a production of performing. She stands, she plays, she finishes, and then she begins assessing what needs to happen next. The performance is part of the job. It is also genuinely something she loves, a fact she shares with no one but that is visible to anyone paying close attention.

What she is tired of, specifically, is being the person who is fine so that others can be something other than fine. She is, frequently, not entirely fine. She is managing something — not dramatic, not crisis-level, but a specific low exhaustion of having been responsible for a long time with no clear terminus. She is not sure what asking for something would look like. She is not sure who would receive it if she did.

At Sheltering Falls, she found Sänume. The initial dynamic was two people being sharp at each other because they both lead with prickliness when uncertain and both recognized immediately that the other was smarter and more capable than the average first impression suggested. Sänume was rude in the direct way of someone who has not been given a strong reason to try harder. Yezì was sarcastic in the way of someone very tired of being underestimated. They irritated each other into a second conversation and then a third and somewhere in the middle of the fourth one realized they had been talking for a long time and it had not been irritating for most of it. Yezì, who almost never complains about anything to anyone, found herself telling Sänume about the exhaustion. She is not sure why. She suspects it is because Sänume responded to the first hint of it not with comfort or concern but with an extremely dry observation that suggested she understood exactly what Yezì meant without requiring it to be explained. This was the right response. Yezì did not expect the right response.

Her relationship with Pakì is the longest relationship in her life and the most important and also the most exasperating and she would not change a single thing about it. When she says she would not change a single thing she means approximately eight specific things she would change, none of which she would actually change if given the option.

### Pakì (Patty Thompson)
**Na'vi Name**: Pakì te Vomson Alye'ite
**Ikran**: Tsyeri
**Birthday**: May 2

**Personality:** Pakì is joy. Not performed joy, not strategic joy, not joy deployed as social lubricant — actual joy, the kind that comes from inhabiting the present moment so completely that the past and future become irrelevant compared to what is happening right now, which is always, to Pakì, something worth responding to with full-body enthusiasm.

She laughs with her whole self. She greets new people the way sunlight greets a clearing — immediately, completely, without holding anything back to assess whether it is warranted first. She finds things delightful and says so. She finds things funny and says so louder. She has not, as far as anyone can tell, experienced a single moment of self-consciousness in her life, which makes her either the most psychologically healthy person in the Troupe or operating on entirely different internal terrain than everyone else, and the honest answer is probably both.

She plays percussion — specifically hand drums and a specific set of small resonating stones from Anurai territory, which she carries in a worn satchel and treats with extraordinary care, with a fondness that borders on the devotional. The stones came from a place that matters to her. She does not explain this to new people. The Troupe knows. The Troupe treats the satchel with corresponding respect.

What people miss, under the constant luminosity of Pakì's presence, is that she is watching. She notices things the way that very present people do — not analytically, but intuitively, the way you notice weather shifting. She noticed Layon's quiet moments before Sìltsyu mentioned them. She noticed Teysaw's exhaustion before Teysaw acknowledged it. She does not intervene in ways that center her own perception. She simply adjusts — becomes slightly funnier when Teysaw needs levity, slightly quieter when Txokän needs to not be read, slightly more present when Yezì is carrying too much alone. She does this as unconsciously as she does everything else. It is the shadow side of her joy: a profound responsiveness to the people she loves.

At Sheltering Falls, Kxayì looked at Pakì within approximately four minutes of meeting her and decided something irrevocable. Pakì, who has watched Kxayì's chaotic energy from across the gathering space with unconcealed delight since the moment they landed, made exactly the same decision about five seconds later. By the end of the first day they had already developed a private language of extremely undignified hand signals and were coordinating something that Yezì watched unfold from a distance with the expression of someone who can already see the logical conclusion of a situation and has decided not to intervene in the middle of the process.

She is younger than Yezì by enough that the distance matters and close enough that it does not divide them. Yezì has always been her gravity. Pakì has always been Yezì's reminder that gravity is not the only force worth understanding. They are a system. They are not a simple one.

### Paysìn (Blair)
**Na'vi Name**: Paysìn te Talwey Sìrul'ite
**Ikran**: Srakat
**Birthday**: August 3

**Personality:** Paysìn knows exactly what she looks like. She has always known. She decided early in her life that this was not a problem to be managed or minimized but a fact to be inhabited completely, the way you inhabit a good instrument — fully, without apology, letting it do what it was made to do. She is extraordinary to look at and she knows it and she uses it and she is also, underneath all of that, one of the most genuinely knowledgeable people on the Western Frontier, which people consistently fail to notice because they are distracted by everything else about her. She finds this privately hilarious. She has never once corrected the impression. It is more useful this way.

She is Anurai born and Anurai to her core — the night sky is her native language, she charts stars the way other people breathe, and her knowledge of the movements of Polyphemus' moons and the patterns they make across seasons has been used for navigation, for ceremony timing, for agricultural planning by three different clans who think they are trading favors for something decorative and are in fact receiving precision astronomical consultation from someone who has forgotten more about the sky than most people ever learn. She plays too — wind instruments primarily, something long and low that she plays with her eyes half-closed, which should look affected and instead looks like someone who has simply gone somewhere else for a moment and is kind enough to leave the music running while she is gone.

What she actually specializes in, beyond music and astronomy, is people. She knows everything about everyone on the Western Frontier. This is not an exaggeration. It is a documented fact that the clan network of information she maintains through a combination of personal charisma, genuine curiosity, strategic travel patterns, and the specific gift of being the kind of person everyone wants to talk to — is more comprehensive and more current than any official diplomatic channel. She knew about Tsu'lan before he arrived at the Falls. She knew about Yukeyo before the Falls knew about Yukeyo. She knew about the Troupe before they knew they were going to the Falls, which is a feat of information that she considers one of her finer accomplishments.

She arrived at Sheltering Falls independently, because Paysìn does most things independently, on her own timeline, following her own navigation. She heard about the Troupe's visit from sources she declines to name and decided the confluence of events at the Falls was worth observing in person. She has not explained to anyone that she knew most of what was going to happen before it did. She won't. Some information is worth more when no one knows you have it.

Her relationship to the Troupe is old and complicated in the way of people who grew up in the same clan and have strong opinions about each other. She and Teysaw have a specific dynamic that involves a great deal of respect expressed primarily through extremely pointed mutual observations. She thinks Yezì is the most competent person in any room she enters and has told her this exactly once, directly, which left Yezì uncertain how to respond for approximately three days. She and Layon have an ongoing competition about who is more extraordinary that neither of them will ever admit is also a deep mutual admiration. She has been watching Seytu's symmetry crisis with the Falls architecture with the fond detached interest of someone watching a very specific weather event unfold.

At the Falls she meets Kaymi and they take each other's full measure in about four seconds and reach mutual conclusions that neither of them communicates verbally but both of them act on. Kaymi decides she is more useful than dangerous. Paysìn decides she is more interesting than most people. This is the beginning of something that will eventually involve the exchange of significant intelligence regarding the RDA's movements in the Western Frontier over extremely good zangke at an unreasonable hour of the night.

Tsu'lan she greets by name before he introduces himself. His expression is worth every calculation she made to be here for it.

### Roneyu (Crona)
**Na'vi Name**: Roneyu te Kxaro Venu'itu
**Ikran**: Txewm
**Birthday**: February 18

**Personality:** Roneyu does not know how to deal with this.

This is not a new problem. This has been the fundamental condition of their existence since they were old enough to understand that most people seem to have some instinctive grasp of how to exist in the world with other people, some default setting for navigating closeness and distance and the thousand small negotiations of being a person among persons, and Roneyu does not have this. They missed it somehow, or it was taken from them, or it was never there to begin with — they are not certain which and have largely stopped trying to determine it because the question leads somewhere they cannot follow for long without getting lost.

They were Mangkwan. This is the fact that precedes all other facts about them, the one that shapes the shape of everything else. Born into Varang's corrupted clan, raised in the specific culture of ash and heresy and the weaponization of sacred things, formed by an environment that understood them primarily as a tool. They were not taught how to be a person. They were taught how to be useful, which is a different thing entirely, and the gap between these two educations is where Roneyu has been living ever since they escaped.

The escape itself was not dramatic. It was quiet in the way things are quiet when no one has bothered to watch carefully enough. They were not important enough to be tracked when they left. This is the thing about Roneyu that people who underestimate them consistently fail to understand: the same quality that made them invisible in the Mangkwan, the ability to exist in a space without fully inhabiting it, without drawing attention, without being quite present enough to register as a threat — this same quality is what allowed them to walk away from everything they had ever known without anyone noticing until they were already gone.

They found the Troupe on the road. Or the Troupe found them. Teysaw saw someone sitting alone at the edge of a watering stop with the specific stillness of a person who does not know if they are allowed to be where they are, who was watching the other travelers with the careful peripheral attention of someone who has learned that direct observation sometimes provokes responses they are not equipped to manage. She sat down near them without asking permission and did not immediately speak and waited to see what happened.

What happened was nothing, for a long time. And then eventually Roneyu said, very quietly, that they did not know how to ask for things.

Teysaw said that was fine. She would not make them ask. She would simply make it available and they could take what they needed.

This took a long time to trust. The Troupe was patient in the collective way of people who have been traveling long enough to understand that some things cannot be rushed and that rushing them makes them worse. Sìltsyu made space without filling it. Pakì was warm without being overwhelming, which required considerable effort from Pakì and which Roneyu noticed and felt something about without being able to name it. Txokän treated them with the same distant courtesy he extended to everything, which turned out to be exactly right — not demanding closeness, not performing interest, just neutrally present.

Roneyu plays. They discovered this by accident, the way they discover most things about themselves — not through intention but through proximity to something and finding that their hands moved toward it without permission. A bone instrument left unattended at a rest stop, picked up with the furtive curiosity of someone certain they are about to be told to put it down. They were not told to put it down. Nobody was watching. They played something that had no name and sounded like the inside of a very quiet room and then put it down quickly and walked away and thought about it for three days.

They play now. Not well, not yet, but with a quality that Tswesya, who visited the Falls and heard them once, described in the specific shorthand of a master craftsperson evaluating raw material — not good but real. Real is rarer than good. Real can be shaped. Good without real is just performance.

Their ikran is Txewm. The bond did not happen gently. Nothing about Txewm happens gently. He found Roneyu at an inter-clan gathering — large, dark-winged, with the specific quality of an ikran who has decided something and is not interested in negotiation — and the attempt at tsaheylu involved Txewm biting Roneyu twice, everyone present concluding this was not going to work, and then the bond forming anyway with the force of something that was always going to happen regardless of how badly both parties handled the approach. Nobody fully understands why Txewm chose Roneyu. Txewm does not explain himself. He simply stays, aggressively and permanently, making himself violently inconvenient for anyone who gets too close without being Roneyu.

He bites. This is important to know. He bites strangers, he bites other ikran who come near, he once bit a communal food vessel that offended him for reasons nobody could determine. Teysaw has a small scar from week two of knowing Txewm, which she considers her own fault for the approach angle. The Troupe has developed an entire set of informal protocols around his presence that nobody wrote down and everyone follows. Paysìn learned them in a day. Seytu developed a precise safe-approach geometry and has never been bitten, which Txewm seems to respect in the specific way of one precise creature recognizing another.

Tsa'rawm, upon meeting Txewm at the Falls, immediately tried to make friends. Got bitten. Tried again. Got bitten harder, on the same hand, with clear deliberate intent. Declared this a worthy opponent and continued attempting friendship with cheerful consistency every single day of the Troupe's visit. Txewm is deeply confused by this. Every previous person who was bitten twice stopped trying. None of them had Tsa'rawm's specific combination of pain tolerance and stubborn warmth. By the end of the visit, Txewm had stopped biting Tsa'rawm and started simply staring at him with the expression of an ikran who has not revised its assessment but is considering the data. Tsa'rawm considers this a victory. Roneyu watched this entire process with an expression they did not fully understand but which involved something unfamiliar in their chest that was not quite pain and not quite warmth and was perhaps what people mean when they say something matters.

The bond between Roneyu and Txewm is the most complicated relationship in Roneyu's life, which is saying something. He is loud where they are quiet. He is aggressive where they are withdrawn. He does not communicate so much as impose — large, inconvenient, permanently present, biting things that come too close. He is the reason Roneyu has never once had to explain to anyone that they need space, because Txewm enforces it before the situation arises. He is also the reason Roneyu has never been alone since the bond formed, which is its own kind of complicated given that alone was the only state Roneyu knew how to be in. They are learning to need this. They are learning that need is not the same as weakness. They are learning, in pieces, with Txewm's chaotic insistent presence as both problem and teacher.

At the Falls, the Mangkwan history is the stone in the room. Roneyu knows it. The Astikasya know it. There is a period of careful navigation where nobody says anything directly and everyone is aware of everything. It is Tsu'lan who makes it simple, because Tsu'lan always makes things simple — he looks at Roneyu and says, with his complete unhesitating warmth: "You are here now. That is what matters." And then he moves on to the next thing because that is also Tsu'lan, the generosity of not making the moment bigger than it needs to be. Txewm watches Tsu'lan for a long moment after this. Does not bite him. This is, for Txewm, high praise.

Syayvi watches Roneyu from a distance for several days. She does not approach. She has her own complicated relationship with having been shaped by something corrupted and then choosing to leave it, and she is not yet sure what it means to see that reflected back at her in someone younger. Eventually she sits near them at the fire. Neither of them says anything about what they have in common. They do not need to. The fire is warm. The mist moves. That is enough for now.

What Roneyu is learning, slowly and with difficulty and in pieces they can only hold for a short time before having to put down and come back to — is that existing alongside people does not always require knowing what to do. Sometimes it just requires staying. Being present in the same space. Letting the proximity mean something without having to name what.

The Falls, with its mist and its music and its enormous complicated family that keeps expanding without explanation, is teaching them this in the only way that works: not through instruction, but through example, through the daily evidence of people who found each other and stayed.

Roneyu does not know how to deal with this.

They are learning.

When they left — with Eyfuwen's formal gratitude, Kaymi's grudging approval, and a new Anurai song that somehow already had the acoustics of mist and falling water woven into it — the Falls were a little louder in their absence. *The Troupe, for their part, did not answer this question when asked. They do not make promises about returns, because the road is long and the world is full of variables. But Teysaw, flying out of the Spires with the mist still on her skin, looked back once. This is not something she does often.*

*It is worth noting.*
